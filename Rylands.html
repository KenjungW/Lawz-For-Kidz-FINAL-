Rylands v Fletcher is a foundational case in tort law, establishing the doctrine of strict liability for damage caused by dangerous things escaping from one's land — holding landowners responsible regardless of fault or negligence.

Facts

In 1860, Rylands paid contractors to build a reservoir on his land for a mill. They later noticed old coal shafts under the land that were connected to Thomas Fletcher’s mine but ignored it. Shortly after the reservoir was filled, it burst and flooded Fletcher’s mine. The Court of Exchequer initially ruled for Rylands. On appeal, the Court of Exchequer Chamber reversed it, holding Rylands strictly liable.

Arguments

Fletcher argued that Rylands should be held liable as the flooding violated his right to enjoyment and safe possession of land. Fletcher also stated that storing mass volumes of water is considered “dangerous” and “non-natural” use of land. Rylands argued that he shouldn’t be held liable due to having no knowledge of the coal shafts and that the escaped water was out of his foresight.

Judgement

The House of Lords ruled in favor of Fletcher, holding Rylands liable despite him not being directly at fault or negligible. Justice Blackburn states that as Rylands is utilizing land in an unnatural manner, he is liable for all damages and does so at his own peril.

Impact

Rylands v Fletcher established strict liability, holding landowners responsible for damage caused by the escape of dangerous things brought onto their property, regardless of negligence. This landmark ruling continues to shape modern environmental laws and court liability for dangerous industrial activities.

Quick Facts

Year: 1868

Court: House of Lords

Legal Area: Tort (Strict Liability)

Key Principle: Strict Liability

Outcome: Fletcher won; Rylands held strictly liable
